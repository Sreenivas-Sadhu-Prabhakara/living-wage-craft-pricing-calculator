How it works

Most craft pricing starts from materials and stops there. This one starts from your hours. Materials for the whole batch are grossed up by wastage, because offcuts and spoiled dye are material you paid for and cannot sell. Money you actually handed to helpers or job-workers is added as it stands. Your monthly overhead — rent, power, tools, packing, travel to the market — is divided by the number of batches you make in a month, so each batch carries its share. Those three together are the cash that left your hands. Your own time is deliberately kept out of that figure.

Seconds are taken off the piece count, not off the materials: you bought clay or thread for the whole batch, so the cost of the batch has to be carried by the pieces you can actually sell. Making hours and unpaid hours are added together — sourcing, packing, photographs, answering buyers and keeping accounts are work, and leaving them out is the most common reason a maker's price is too low.

The headline number is then simple arithmetic: take the revenue your current price would earn on the saleable pieces, subtract the cash you paid out, and divide what is left by your own hours. That is the hourly wage you are paying yourself, whatever your margin says. It is compared with the benchmark you entered — a daily minimum or living wage divided by the hours in your working day — and if it falls short, the page says so and shows how much more each piece has to carry.

In short: cash out = materials × (1 + wastage%) + helpers + overhead ÷ batches, saleable = pieces × (1 − seconds%), your hourly rate = (price × saleable − cash out) ÷ (making hours + unpaid hours). The price bands run the other way: full cost = (cash out + hours × your target wage) ÷ saleable, then wholesale = full cost × (1 + margin%), with retail and export as a markup and an uplift on wholesale.

Assumptions & rates
  • Wastage grosses up materials only. It never touches helpers, job-work or overhead.
  • Seconds reduce the saleable pieces, not the materials, so the whole batch cost is carried by what you can sell. Seconds are treated as unsellable, not as discounted B-grade stock.
  • Monthly overhead is divided by the batches you make in a month. If either figure is blank, no overhead is allocated rather than dividing by zero.
  • Making hours and unpaid hours are added together and costed at the hourly wage you set for yourself.
  • Benchmark hourly wage = daily wage ÷ hours in a working day. Both are yours to enter; minimum wages in India and the Philippines are published as daily figures.
  • Indicative placeholder figures only, shown until you type your own: —; —. These are round numbers for illustration, not current, authoritative or region-specific rates.
  • Starting conventions, all editable: margin — on full cost; retail at — over wholesale, the long-standing craft practice that stops a maker undercutting the shops that stock them; export uplift — over wholesale as a buffer for documentation, forwarding and currency movement.
  • A margin below −100% is clamped to −100%, so a price never goes negative. Negative entries for money, hours and piece counts are read as zero.
  • GST, VAT, income tax, marketplace commission, payment charges and shipping to the buyer are not included. Add them on top of the band you choose.
An estimate to price with, not a wage determination or legal advice.
